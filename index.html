1. Beautiful Flowers

Flowers are the beautiful and reproductive parts of flowering plants. They show different colours, shapes and sizes.

Introduction to Flowers

A flower is the reproductive structure of an angiosperm. It helps plants reproduce and produce seeds.

Importance of Flowers

  • Flowers help in plant reproduction.
  • They attract pollinators.
  • They produce seeds and fruits.
  • Many flowers are used for decoration and perfumes.

2. Reproduction in Plants

Reproduction is the biological process by which plants produce new plants of their own kind.

Definition of Reproduction

Reproduction is the process by which living organisms produce offspring. In plants, reproduction may be sexual or asexual.

Types of Reproduction

  • Sexual Reproduction: Involves the fusion of male and female gametes.
  • Asexual Reproduction: A new plant develops from one parent without fusion of gametes.

3. Structure of a Flower

A typical flower consists of four main whorls arranged on the receptacle.

Four Main Whorls

  • Calyx – made up of sepals.
  • Corolla – made up of petals.
  • Androecium – male reproductive part.
  • Gynoecium – female reproductive part.

Receptacle and Pedicel

The receptacle is the swollen tip of the flower stalk where floral parts are attached. The pedicel is the stalk that supports the flower.

4. Parts of a Flower and Their Functions

Each part of a flower performs a special function.

Sepals

Sepals are usually green, leaf-like structures. They protect the flower when it is in the bud stage.

Petals

Petals are usually colourful and help attract insects and other pollinators.

Stamen

The stamen is the male reproductive part of a flower. It consists of anther and filament.

Pistil

The pistil is the female reproductive part. It consists of stigma, style and ovary.

5. Male Reproductive Part (Stamen)

The stamen is the male reproductive organ of a flower.

Structure of Stamen

A typical stamen consists of two main parts: anther and filament.

Anther

The anther is the upper part of the stamen. It produces pollen grains that contain the male gametophyte.

Filament

The filament is a slender stalk that supports the anther and holds it in a suitable position.

Pollen Grains

Pollen grains are produced in the anther. They carry the male gametophyte involved in fertilization.

6. Female Reproductive Part (Pistil/Carpel)

The pistil or carpel is the female reproductive organ of a flower.

Structure of Pistil

The pistil consists of three main parts: stigma, style and ovary.

Stigma

The stigma is the upper part of the pistil. It receives pollen grains during pollination.

Style

The style connects the stigma with the ovary. It provides a pathway for the pollen tube.

Ovary and Ovule

The ovary contains ovules. After fertilization, the ovary usually develops into a fruit and the ovules develop into seeds.

7. Pollination

Pollination is an important step in the sexual reproduction of flowering plants.

Definition of Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of a flower.

Importance of Pollination

  • It helps in sexual reproduction.
  • It allows pollen to reach the stigma.
  • It makes fertilization possible.
  • It helps in seed and fruit formation.

8. Types of Pollination

Pollination is mainly divided into two types.

Self-Pollination

Self-pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant.

Examples: Pea and wheat.

Cross-Pollination

Cross-pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of one plant to the stigma of a flower on another plant of the same species.

Examples: Apple and papaya.

9. Agents of Pollination

Agents of pollination help transfer pollen grains from anther to stigma.

Insects

Bees, butterflies and other insects carry pollen grains while visiting flowers for nectar.

Wind

Wind carries light pollen grains from one flower to another. Many grasses are wind-pollinated.

Water

In some aquatic plants, water helps transfer pollen grains.

Animals

Birds and some other animals help pollinate flowers while feeding on nectar.

10. Fertilization in Flowering Plants

Fertilization is the process in which male and female gametes fuse to form a zygote.

Definition of Fertilization

Fertilization is the fusion of a male gamete with a female gamete. In flowering plants, it takes place inside the embryo sac of the ovule.

Pollen Tube Formation

After pollination, a pollen grain germinates on the stigma and forms a pollen tube. The tube grows through the style towards the ovule.

11. Double Fertilization in Flowering Plants

Double fertilization is a special feature of flowering plants. It involves two fusion events inside the embryo sac.

1. Definition

Two male gametes take part in two separate fusion events inside the embryo sac. These events are called syngamy and triple fusion.

2. Pollen Tube Formation

The pollen tube grows from the pollen grain through the style towards the ovule. It carries two male gametes.

3. Entry into Ovule

The pollen tube usually enters the ovule through the micropyle and releases the two male gametes into the embryo sac.

4. Syngamy

One male gamete fuses with the egg cell to form a diploid zygote (2n). The zygote develops into an embryo.

5. Triple Fusion

The second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei to form a triploid primary endosperm nucleus (3n).

Importance of Double Fertilization

It leads to the formation of the embryo and endosperm. The endosperm provides nourishment to the developing embryo.

12. Development of Endosperm in Flowering Plants

Endosperm is a nutritive tissue that provides food to the developing embryo.

1. Definition of Endosperm

Endosperm is a food-storing tissue that usually develops from the primary endosperm nucleus after triple fusion. It is generally triploid (3n).

2. Formation of Endosperm

The second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei. The resulting primary endosperm nucleus divides repeatedly to form endosperm.

3. Types of Endosperm

  • Nuclear: Nuclear divisions occur without immediate cell wall formation.
  • Cellular: Cell walls form after nuclear divisions.
  • Helobial: The first division forms two chambers.

4. Functions of Endosperm

  • Stores food for the embryo.
  • Provides nutrients during development.
  • Supports early seed development.

5. Examples of Endosperm

Maize, wheat and rice contain food-storing endosperm. Coconut water is liquid endosperm, while its white edible part is solid endosperm.

Conclusion

Endosperm develops after triple fusion and provides nourishment to the developing embryo.

13. Development of Embryo in Flowering Plants

After fertilization, the zygote develops into an embryo inside the ovule.

1. Formation of Zygote

The fusion of a male gamete with the egg cell forms a diploid zygote (2n).

2. First Division of Zygote

The zygote divides by mitosis. In many dicot plants, the first division produces a terminal cell and a basal cell.

3. Formation of Suspensor

The basal cell usually develops into the suspensor, which helps position the developing embryo.

4. Stages of Embryo Development

In many dicot plants, the embryo passes through globular, heart-shaped and torpedo stages.

5. Mature Embryo

A mature embryo generally contains a radicle, plumule, cotyledons and an embryonic axis.

Conclusion

The zygote develops through repeated cell divisions and differentiation to form the embryo of a new plant.

14. Formation of Seed and Fruit

After fertilization, the ovule and ovary develop into seed and fruit respectively.

Formation of Seed

After fertilization, the ovule develops into a seed. The integuments usually form the seed coat.

Formation of Fruit

After fertilization, the ovary usually develops into a fruit. The ovary wall forms the fruit wall, called the pericarp.

Structure of Seed

A typical seed contains a seed coat, an embryo and stored food or nutritive tissue.

15. Types and Structure of Seeds

Seeds are mainly classified into monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous seeds.

Monocot Seed

A monocot seed has one cotyledon. Examples include maize, rice and wheat.

Dicot Seed

A dicot seed has two cotyledons. Examples include bean, pea and gram.

16. Seed Germination and Its Stages

Seed germination is the process by which a seed develops into a young plant under suitable conditions.

1. Absorption of Water

The seed absorbs water. This process is called imbibition and activates the seed.

2. Breaking of Seed Coat

As the seed absorbs water, it swells and the seed coat breaks open.

3. Growth of Radicle

The radicle emerges first and develops into the primary root.

4. Growth of Plumule

The plumule develops into the shoot system of the young plant.

5. Formation of Seedling

The young plant develops its first leaves and gradually becomes a seedling.

Conditions Required for Germination

  • Water or moisture
  • Oxygen
  • Suitable temperature
